Two Sets of Books: What the Kalder Case Teaches Seed Investors and Founders

Steve HaytonNovember 20264 min read

Kalder was a New York startup building loyalty and rewards programs for brands. Its founder, Gökçe Güven, was on the Forbes 30 Under 30 list. In April 2024 she started raising a seed round, and the deck she showed investors was a strong one: 26 brands “using Kalder,” another 53 in “live freemium,” and recurring revenue that climbed every month from February 2023 to $1.2M in annual recurring revenue by March 2024.

More than a dozen investors put in about $7M. At the end of October 2026 she was sentenced to 25 months in federal prison.

How it worked

According to federal prosecutors, some of the brands on that slide were in short, heavily discounted pilots. Others had no agreement with Kalder at all. The company had an outside accounting firm that kept its real financials. Güven kept a second set, with inflated numbers, and that was the set investors saw.

There was no hack and no network of shell companies. Just a spreadsheet with different numbers in it.

Prosecutors also say the same claims went into an O-1A visa application, the visa for people of “extraordinary ability,” along with reference letters that appeared to be signed by business executives but that she had signed herself. That visa has since been revoked. Her guilty plea, in May 2026, was to one count of securities fraud. She was also ordered to pay about $6.7M each in forfeiture and restitution and, without admitting the SEC’s allegations, agreed to a six-year ban on serving as an officer or director.

Why smart people missed it

I’m not writing this to pile on the investors. The real numbers existed the whole time, but in a moving round, with other funds already in and a founder carrying a Forbes badge, asking for raw bank data can feel like an accusation. That social pressure is exactly what this kind of fraud relies on.

Where do these numbers come from?

Before venture I was an investigative journalist, and my time working on the Volkswagen emissions scandal taught me one question. It was never “are these numbers impressive?” It was “where do these numbers come from?”

In a seed round, that question is cheap to ask. Revenue can be seen at the source, in the bank account or the payment processor, rather than in a spreadsheet. The people who keep the books can be called. Customers can be picked off the slide rather than handed over as references. And a revenue line that rises every single month for more than a year at a seed-stage company deserves a second look, because real early revenue is lumpy.

A founder with real numbers usually says yes to any of that in minutes.

For founders

The lesson is simpler. Every number in your deck is one someone will eventually check. Write the version you’d be comfortable having read line by line.
